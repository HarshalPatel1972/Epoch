Chapter 1

Meet Mara.

Mara runs a small online shop: keyboards, monitors, desk chairs. In six months it took just over two thousand orders. Her app decides each one. Is there stock? What's the price? Is there a discount?

Chapter 2

One Monday, a question.

Her partner wants a loyalty discount. It sounds sensible. But would it have paid for itself, or just given money away?

Chapter 3

Her database can't say.

It stores what is: today's stock, the orders that went through. The 48 orders turned away for lack of stock left no trace at all. Every UPDATE wrote over the state the next decision depended on.

Chapter 4

So the answer becomes a guess.

A spreadsheet. Revenue times some share, times fifteen percent. Nobody knows how many customers would have qualified, or how a cheaper price would have drained stock that later orders needed.

Chapter 5

Epoch keeps the question.

With Epoch, Mara's app records every command it receives, accepted or rejected, along with the decision it made. Nothing is overwritten. It's a log of everything she was ever asked.

Chapter 6

Then it replays the past under the new rule.

Epoch forks history at 1 January, a branch next to the real timeline, and sends every recorded order back through the new pricing logic. In order, at the original time, with the stock that existed then.

Chapter 7

Decisions ripple forward.

Try allowing up to three backorders. A 10-chair order on 20 May, refused in real life, now goes through. Stock drops below zero, and five chair orders that really were fulfilled, the last one weeks later on 8 June, get refused instead. The big sale loses money overall.

A spreadsheet can't follow that chain. A replay follows it every time, because each decision sees exactly the world the earlier ones left behind.

Chapter 8

Not a guess. An answer.

The discount would have cost $43,416, spread across 1,477 orders, each one listed. No rejected order would have been saved. Mara's real data was never touched.

The idea, in Go

Two pure functions. Everything else follows.

You write your business logic as Decide (command → events, or a refusal) and Evolve (state + event → state). Epoch records, versions, branches and replays.

How it compares

    
Good company. A different question.

    
Many tools can show you the past. Epoch is the one built to re-decide it. It's a library that runs alongside your database, not a replacement for one.

Fine print, up front

    
What Epoch won't do.

  

  

    
Predict behaviour
A replay re-runs the requests you actually received. It can't know that a 15% discount would have brought in customers who never showed up.

    
Fix non-determinism
Decisions must depend only on state, the command and the time. Put prices, scores and exchange rates in the command so they're recorded.

    
Replace your database
Epoch stores its log in a store you choose: in-memory, SQLite today, or your own via a 37-test conformance suite.

    
Scale out on its own
The SQLite store fits one application node. A Postgres store is next on the roadmap. Epoch is beta: pin your version.
